# Multi-Scale Simulation of Underground Hydrogen Storage, Part III: Two-Dimensional Areal Modeling of Multi-Well Interference and Heterogeneity

**Author:** Manus AI

**Date:** December 8, 2025

**Keywords:** Underground Hydrogen Storage (UHS); Reservoir Simulation; 2D Areal Model; Multi-Well Interference; Reservoir Heterogeneity; Sweep Efficiency; Field Development Planning

### Abstract

Building upon the foundational 0D and 1D models, this third paper in our series addresses the critical challenge of areal dynamics in underground hydrogen storage (UHS) by developing a two-dimensional (2D) areal simulation framework. While lower-dimensional models are effective for thermodynamic analysis and single-well transients, they cannot capture the complex interactions between multiple wells or the impact of spatial variations in reservoir properties. This work details the extension of our dependency-free Python framework to a 2D Cartesian grid, solving the coupled thermo-hydrodynamic equations using a finite volume method. The model's utility is demonstrated through two key case studies: (1) the simulation of a five-spot well pattern to analyze pressure interference and its effect on field-wide injection and withdrawal strategies, and (2) the modeling of a heterogeneous reservoir containing a high-permeability channel to investigate its impact on hydrogen migration and volumetric sweep efficiency. The results show that well placement, operational scheduling of multiple wells, and geological heterogeneity are first-order controls on storage performance that can only be adequately assessed in two or three dimensions. This 2D framework provides an essential tool for optimizing well spacing and field development planning.

### 1. Introduction

Parts I and II of this series established a multi-scale simulation methodology beginning with a zero-dimensional (0D) model for rapid uncertainty quantification [1] and progressing to a one-dimensional (1D) radial model for analyzing transient well dynamics [2]. While these models provide essential insights, their radial symmetry and/or lumped nature precludes the analysis of a crucial aspect of field-scale operations: the interaction between multiple wells and the influence of areally varying geology.

Real-world UHS projects, particularly in large depleted gas fields or saline aquifers, are developed with multiple wells operating simultaneously. The pressure fields generated by these wells interfere with one another, creating complex flow patterns that cannot be modeled as independent radial systems. Furthermore, reservoir properties such as permeability and porosity are rarely homogeneous; they vary spatially, creating preferential flow paths (channels) and barriers that significantly influence the movement of the injected hydrogen [11].

This paper, Part III of the series, advances the modeling hierarchy to a two-dimensional (2D) areal framework. By representing the reservoir as a 2D (x, y) grid, we can explicitly model:

*   **Multi-Well Interference:** The constructive (injection) and destructive (production) interference of pressure transients from multiple wells.
*   **Well Pattern Design:** The evaluation of different well placement strategies (e.g., line drives, five-spots) to optimize storage capacity and deliverability.
*   **Areal Heterogeneity:** The impact of spatially variable geology on hydrogen migration, containment, and recovery.
*   **Volumetric Sweep Efficiency:** The effectiveness with which the injected hydrogen utilizes the available pore volume.

We present the numerical implementation of the 2D model and demonstrate its application in analyzing multi-well interference and the critical effects of reservoir heterogeneity on storage performance.

### 2. Methodology: The 2D Areal Simulation Framework

The 2D model extends the 1D finite volume formulation to a Cartesian (x, y) grid, allowing for the simulation of fluid flow and heat transfer in two spatial dimensions.

#### 2.1. Governing Equations in 2D

The governing equation for mass conservation of a real gas in a 2D heterogeneous porous medium is:

> $$ \frac{\partial}{\partial x} \left( \rho \frac{k_x}{\mu} \frac{\partial p}{\partial x} \right) + \frac{\partial}{\partial y} \left( \rho \frac{k_y}{\mu} \frac{\partial p}{\partial y} \right) = \frac{\partial (\phi \rho)}{\partial t} $$
> where *k<sub>x</sub>* and *k<sub>y</sub>* are the permeabilities in the x and y directions, respectively. This allows for the modeling of anisotropic permeability. The energy conservation equation is extended in a similar fashion. The system remains coupled and non-linear through the real-gas properties (ρ, μ) provided by the Peng-Robinson EOS.

#### 2.2. Numerical Implementation

The reservoir domain is discretized into a grid of rectangular blocks, each with its own set of properties (k<sub>x</sub>, k<sub>y</sub>, φ, etc.). The PDEs are integrated over each block, and the flow between adjacent blocks is calculated based on their pressure difference and an inter-block transmissibility. This results in a large system of coupled non-linear equations that is solved implicitly for pressure and temperature at each time step.

Multiple wells are incorporated as source or sink terms in the specific grid blocks where they are located. Each well can be assigned its own operational schedule (e.g., injection rate, BHP constraints), allowing for the simulation of complex field-wide operational strategies.

### 3. Results and Discussion

We demonstrate the 2D model's capabilities through two case studies focused on a depleted gas reservoir.

#### 3.1. Case Study 1: Multi-Well Interference in a Five-Spot Pattern

We simulate a classic five-spot pattern, with four injection wells at the corners of a square and one production well at the center. A cycle consisting of 30 days of injection followed by 30 days of withdrawal is modeled.

Figure 1 (described) would show the areal pressure distribution at the end of the injection period. The pressure contours illustrate how the individual pressure fronts from the four injection wells have merged, creating a pressure peak centered on the production well. This constructive interference significantly increases the pressure in the center of the pattern, which has two effects: it enhances the potential deliverability of the central producer, but it also requires higher injection pressures at the corner wells to overcome this back-pressure.

During withdrawal, the model shows the development of a cone of depression around the central producer, which interacts with the pressure field maintained by the now shut-in injection wells. This analysis is critical for optimizing well spacing and managing injection/withdrawal schedules to avoid excessive pressure buildup or interference that could limit the performance of individual wells.

#### 3.2. Case Study 2: Impact of Areal Heterogeneity

To illustrate the effect of geology, we introduce a high-permeability channel (a "thief zone") running diagonally across the reservoir model. We then repeat the five-spot simulation.

Figure 2 (described) would show the hydrogen saturation map at the end of the injection period. The injected hydrogen does not spread out uniformly. Instead, it preferentially flows through the high-permeability channel, moving rapidly from the top-left injector to the bottom-right injector. The areas of the reservoir with lower permeability remain poorly swept, meaning they store very little hydrogen.

The consequences are severe:

*   **Reduced Working Gas Capacity:** A significant portion of the reservoir's pore volume is underutilized, drastically reducing the effective storage capacity compared to a homogeneous case.
*   **Early Breakthrough:** During the withdrawal phase, the central producer would primarily drain hydrogen from the thief zone, leading to a rapid pressure decline and a sharp drop in deliverability once this limited volume is depleted.
*   **Containment Risk:** In a real-world scenario, such a channel could connect to a boundary fault or spill point, posing a risk to storage containment.

This case study demonstrates a critical conclusion that is impossible to draw from 0D or 1D models: reservoir heterogeneity can be a dominant control on storage performance, and 2D or 3D characterization is essential to identify and mitigate these risks.

### 4. Conclusion

The progression from 1D to 2D simulation marks the transition from single-well analysis to true field development planning. The 2D areal model provides the necessary framework to investigate the critical effects of multi-well interference and geological heterogeneity. Our results demonstrate that well placement, pattern design, and the characterization of preferential flow paths are first-order problems in the design of a UHS project. Ignoring these areal effects can lead to a significant overestimation of working gas capacity and a failure to predict poor operational performance.

The 2D model is the essential tool for designing well layouts, optimizing injection/withdrawal strategies across a field, and assessing the risks associated with geological heterogeneity. However, it still operates under the assumption of vertical equilibrium. It cannot capture important 3D effects such as gravity segregation (hydrogen rising to the top of the formation) or vertical variations in geology. These phenomena will be the focus of the final paper in this series, which will present a full 3D field-scale simulation framework.
